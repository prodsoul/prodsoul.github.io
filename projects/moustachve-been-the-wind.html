Moustach’ve Been the Wind

I led an eight-person team building Moustach’ve Been the Wind, a 2D speedrunning platformer. I worked with a programmer to prototype and playtest movement in Unity, built early level blockouts, and made a tool to transfer tested layouts into our custom C++ engine.

Make a run worth repeating

Each run starts in a safe zone. Once the player leaves it, the clock starts and they have to reach the exit before time runs out. On a first attempt, the goal is to make it through. On another run, the same stretch becomes a chance to build speed earlier, land a jump more cleanly, or decide whether a shell is worth the detour.

We wanted the game to feel good in those few seconds between obstacles. That meant thinking about what happened after each move as much as how the move itself felt.

Carry momentum into the next move

I worked with a programmer on the early movement prototype in Unity. One question we kept coming back to was what the player could do immediately after landing or leaving a wall. A move could feel good on its own and still interrupt the run if it left the player having to build speed again.

In the final build, a ground pound leads straight into a roll, carrying the player forward as soon as they land. After a wall kick, the character turns to face the new direction, so the player can keep holding run. Both decisions helped moves connect across a route.

Let the level teach the movement

We gave the first level wide platforms and used coins to draw players forward. The first gap that needs extra speed comes after a stretch where they can build it. That gives the player room to get used to running before the route asks them to judge a jump at speed.

The layout also gives experienced players a reason to look beyond the main path. Shells sit on detours, so players can choose between a quicker finish and a more demanding route. In later levels, we ask for more deliberate run-ups and use vertical sections to put wall slides and wall kicks to work.

Bring tested layouts into the custom engine

Unity gave me a quick way to block out routes and try changes to platform spacing. The final game ran in our custom C++ engine, so we needed a way to carry those layouts over without rebuilding each one by hand.

I built a JSON export tool that transferred tile layers and collision data from Unity. That let the team bring the layout we had tested into the custom engine and continue working on it there.

What I took into the final build

I led the team through six milestones, from the Unity prototype to the final custom-engine build. The project taught me to think about movement and level layout together. A jump’s reach only matters if the player has enough room to build speed before it, and a good landing should set up whatever comes next.

If I tested the game again, I’d watch first-time players on the first speed-dependent gap, then have them replay the same stretch. I’d look at where they start running and whether they can find a cleaner route on their own.
